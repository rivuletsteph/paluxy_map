Data Centers & Your Water

The water people drink, the health of the river, and the watershed they share — what is known about each today, and what a wave of proposed data centers would ask of them. It is one system, so it is really one question.

Start here

Three questions, answered plainly

Most people arrive with the same few questions. Here is the short version — the rest of the page goes deeper.

What's a watershed?

All the land that drains to a common waterway. Rain falling across this area works its way — over the surface and underground — into the Paluxy River. What happens on the land upstream can reach the water everyone downstream depends on.

Is my well part of the river?

Largely, yes. Rain that soaks into the ground recharges the aquifer wells draw from, and that same groundwater feeds the springs holding the river up between storms. Your well and the river are not two separate supplies. They are two places to meet the same water.

How does this affect me?

A large data center takes land and water, and changes how water moves across a site. The thing to watch is not one dramatic event. It is the long-term quality and health of what you drink and what runs past your place, measured against what both are today.

Water quality today

Where the Paluxy stands now

The baseline is the point. Establishing what the river is today is what makes any future change visible — and what an antidegradation review is argued against. This is what is already being measured, by whom, and for how long.

Those 28 TCEQ stations are the formal record — the Clean Rivers Program and SWQMIS sampling that TCEQ assesses the segment against. The Stream Team sites are the volunteer network, and they are where the gap is: ten of the twelve have no results in the public Water Quality Portal. Readings may well have been taken there; they would sit in Stream Team's own database rather than the national one. Pulling them across is a job worth doing, because a volunteer record going back years is exactly the kind of evidence a baseline argument runs on.

What the record already shows

A first pass through that record is done. Paluxy River — two monitoring stations works through the full SWQMIS export for Segment 1229: 3,130 results covering 13 December 2006 to 4 May 2026, 37 analytes, at the two stations that carry the long record — 20232 at the Glen Rose low water crossing, sampled by the Brazos River Authority, and 20343 at Bakers Crossing, sampled by the TCEQ regional office. Both run roughly quarterly.

It holds sampling effort by year, the four screening indicators plotted against their criteria lines, every parameter with a Kendall trend and a Theil–Sen slope, seasonal medians, and a chart of how much of each series is non-detect. That last one matters: orthophosphate is non-detect in all 83 results at both stations, and ammonia at 20343 in 88% — those series describe the laboratory method, not the river.

Who is permitted to discharge

EPA's ECHO database lists 122 facilities in the watershed with a discharge permit on file, current or lapsed. 65 permits are in effect today, and of those, 12 are individual permits — written for one named facility, with limits set for that site. The rest are general permits: a statewide permit covering a category of activity, such as construction stormwater or a concrete batch plant, which a facility joins by filing a notice of intent. The map colours them by type.

Why the Paluxy carries a higher bar

Texas does not hold every stream to the same standard. Under the Texas Surface Water Quality Standards (30 TAC §307) every classified segment is assigned an aquatic life use — minimal, limited, intermediate, high, exceptional, or oyster waters — and its dissolved oxygen criterion follows from that assignment. The higher the use, the higher the bar a discharge has to clear.

Then antidegradation sits on top of all of it. Where a water is already better than its standards require, Tier 2 says that quality has to be kept: a new or increased discharge may not lower it by more than a negligible amount, and may not lower it at all unless the lowering is shown to be necessary. A river in good condition brings that rule with it — which is exactly why it matters to know, and to record, what condition the Paluxy is in now.

To confirm before this goes out. Two things, and they may conflict. (1) Segment 1229's designated uses: a TCEQ permit record lists them as high aquatic life use, public water supply and primary contact recreation, but that document is a few years old and has not been checked against the current Appendix A of §307.10, where the uses and the dissolved oxygen number are actually set. (2) The segment's assessment status: an earlier draft of this page recorded TCEQ listing 1229 as Fully Supporting, while the 303(d) list added it in 2008 for sulfate, total dissolved solids and chloride. Both can be true — different parameters, different years — but the current Integrated Report is what settles it, and until it is read, neither statement should be quoted.

What this means for your water

How water moves — and where development fits

A simple picture of the pathways: rainfall, surface runoff, the river, your well, and the aquifer beneath.

Conceptual diagram — water pathways in the watershed, and where development meets them.

Your well and the river are two places to meet the same water.

That is why this page treats drinking water and river health as one subject. Rain either soaks in or runs off. What soaks in recharges the aquifer that wells draw from, and emerges downhill as the springs and base flow that keep the river alive through a dry August. What runs off goes straight to the channel, carrying whatever the surface was holding, and is gone.

Change that split across enough acres and you change both halves at once: more load arriving in the river, and less stored water underneath to dilute it or to draw on. A well, a spring and a river reach are three windows onto the same body of water.

What paving part of it would do

Roofs, pavement and equipment pads shed rain instead of soaking it up. More of it runs straight to the river, and less soaks in to feed the springs and the base flow that keep the river going between storms. What this has to do with water quality

The estimate above counts water, not pollutants. It belongs here because the two are the same question asked twice.

Runoff off a roof or a parking lot arrives fast and carries what the surface is holding — sediment while the site is being built, then the ordinary load of a paved industrial yard. It does not filter through soil on the way. So a watershed that sheds more of its rain as surface runoff delivers more of that load to the river, in sharper pulses.

The other half matters more, and it is the quieter one. Rain that no longer soaks in is rain that no longer feeds the springs and the base flow. Base flow is what dilutes a discharge and what holds dissolved oxygen up through a Texas summer — and the dissolved oxygen criterion a segment carries is the one that has to hold when flow is at its lowest, which is precisely when there is least water to do the work. Less base flow means the same discharge lands in less water.

Segment 1229's 303(d) listing is for sulfate, total dissolved solids and chloride — constituents that concentrate as flow drops. And Tier 2 antidegradation asks a narrow question: will existing quality be lowered by more than a negligible amount? The paths above are how it could be, without a single new permitted outfall.

This page does not yet address how much water the projects would use. The Tribune's reporting carries per-project figures — Comanche Circle alone lists a one-time flush and fill of 95 million gallons, then 150,000 gallons a day, drawn from on-site groundwater wells. Pumping is a separate question from runoff, and it is not in the estimate above.

Take action

Three practical steps for landowners

Protecting water quality starts with documentation — from establishing your own baseline to engaging the public process.

01

Document your baseline

Before construction begins is the best time to test your water, surface and groundwater both. A documented pre-development record is the most durable reference point you can hold.

TIAER's lab tests drinking water for coliform bacteria and E. coli, and its guidance walks through collecting a sample and getting it to the lab: Drinking water bacteria testing →

  • Register your well with the Upper Trinity Groundwater Conservation District
  • Ask what testing is available, and at what cost
  • Your well may already be on the map — the wells layer comes from the TWDB Groundwater Database, with depth, aquifer, year drilled, and whether levels or quality have ever been recorded there
  • Note USGS gauge 08091500 at Glen Rose for flow comparison
02

Track the permit process

A discharge to the Paluxy requires a TCEQ permit. Where a water is better than its standards require, a Tier 2 antidegradation review applies — and that review is a public document.

  • Watch TCEQ permit notices for this watershed
  • Read the antidegradation analysis when one is filed
  • Note the public comment period dates — they are short
  • Check whether it is an individual or a general permit; only the individual one carries limits written for that site

TCEQ permitting →

03

Engage the process

The Texas system includes formal ways for affected landowners to take part. Written comments become part of the official record.

  • Submit written comment during the comment period
  • Say what you have measured, and when — a dated record carries further than a concern
  • Connect with local watershed organisations
