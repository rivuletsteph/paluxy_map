Data Centers & Your Water

What is known about water quality and the long-term health of the Paluxy River watershed, and what a wave of proposed data centers would ask of it.

Eight data center projects have been proposed in and around Hood County. Four of them sit inside the Paluxy River watershed, along with part of a fifth. This page gathers what is already measured about the river — who samples it, where, and for how long — and sets a first, rough estimate of what paving over part of the watershed would do to the water that reaches it.

It is a starting point for weighing the trade-offs, not a verdict on them.

Why the Paluxy carries a higher bar

Texas does not hold every stream to the same standard. Under the Texas Surface Water Quality Standards (30 TAC §307) every classified segment is assigned an aquatic life use — minimal, limited, intermediate, high, exceptional, or oyster waters — and its dissolved oxygen criterion follows from that assignment. The higher the use, the higher the bar a discharge has to clear.

The Paluxy River and North Paluxy River are Segment 1229 of the Brazos River Basin.

Then antidegradation sits on top of all of it. Where a water is already better than its standards require, Tier 2 says that quality has to be kept: a new or increased discharge may not lower it by more than a negligible amount, and may not lower it at all unless the lowering is shown to be necessary. A river in good condition brings that rule with it — which is exactly why it matters to know, and to record, what condition the Paluxy is in now.

To confirm before this goes out: Segment 1229's designated uses. A TCEQ permit record lists them as high aquatic life use, public water supply and primary contact recreation, but that document is a few years old and has not been checked against the current Appendix A of §307.10, which is where the uses and the dissolved oxygen number are actually set. Worth reading alongside it: Segment 1229 was added to the state's 303(d) list in 2008 for sulfate, total dissolved solids and chloride.

Now vs. with data centers

Roofs, pavement and equipment pads shed rain instead of soaking it up. More of it runs straight to the river, and less of it soaks in to feed the springs and the base flow that keep the river going between storms. This is a screening estimate of that trade, not a model run — move the inputs and watch which way each number goes.

What this has to do with water quality

The estimate above counts water, not pollutants. It belongs here because the two are the same question asked twice.

Runoff off a roof or a parking lot arrives fast and carries what the surface is holding — sediment while the site is being built, then the ordinary load of a paved industrial yard. It does not filter through soil on the way. So a watershed that sheds more of its rain as surface runoff delivers more of that load to the river, in sharper pulses.

The other half matters more, and it is the quieter one. Rain that no longer soaks in is rain that no longer feeds the springs and the base flow. Base flow is what dilutes a discharge and what holds dissolved oxygen up through a Texas summer — and the dissolved oxygen criterion a segment carries is the one that has to hold when flow is at its lowest, which is precisely when there is least water to do the work. Less base flow means the same discharge lands in less water.

Segment 1229 is already on the state's 303(d) list for sulfate, total dissolved solids and chloride — constituents that concentrate as flow drops. And Tier 2 antidegradation asks a narrow question: will existing quality be lowered by more than a negligible amount? The paths above are how it could be, without a single new permitted outfall.
